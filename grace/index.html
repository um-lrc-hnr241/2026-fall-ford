    Read Build 1

    Chosen passage · Elias & Dunning, The Quest for Excitement

    “The range of leisure activities in the more differentiated societies of our age is very wide; differences between them are great. Yet most of them have basic structural characteristics in common. And these common characteristics point to the function they fulfil as leisure activities in societies of a highly differentiated and complex type. While the routines of life in these societies, public or private, demand that people keep a fairly firm hold on their moods and drives, their affects and emotions, leisure occupations as a rule allow them to flow more freely in an imaginary setting specially created by these activities and in some ways reminiscent of non leisure reality. While in the case of the latter the scope for manifestations of feeling is narrowed or confined to special compartments, leisure activities are designed to appeal to people’s feelings directly and to arouse them, though in varying ways and degrees. While excitement is severely curbed in the pursuit of what one usually regards as the serious business of life- apart from sexual excitement which is more strictly confined to privacy - many leisure pursuits provide and imaginary setting which is meant to elicit excitement of some kind imitating that produced by real-life situations, yet without its dangers and risks. Films, dances, paintings, card-games, horse-races, operas, detective stories and football matches; these and many other leisure pursuits belong in this category.

    If one asks how feelings are aroused, excitement is elicited by leisure pursuits, one discovers that it is usually done by the creation of tensions. Imaginary danger, mimetic fear and pleasure, sadness and joy are produced and perhaps resolved by the setting of pastimes. Different moods are evoked and perhaps contrasted, such as sorrow and elation, agitation and peace of mind. Thus the feelings aroused in the imaginary situation of a human leisure activity are the siblings of those aroused in real-life situations—that is what the expression ‘mimetic’ indicates—but the latter are linked to the never-ending risks and perils of fragile human life, while the former momentarily lift the burden of risks and threats, great or small, surrounding human existence” (Elias with Dunning, 42)

    Sports, reading, watching television; after a long day of work, many people choose to engage in these activities. But what role do they fulfil in everyday life? While leisure occupations can take a multitude of different forms, German sociologist Norbert Elias argues that they all perform the same function in developed societies. Specifically, in “The Quest for Excitement: Sport and Leisure in the Civilizing Process”, Elias and another sociologist, Eric Dunning, explain that civilized people gravitate toward certain pastimes because they represent a departure from strict social conventions and provide an outlet for expressing suppressed, subconscious desires.

    Elias’s theory of consciousness acts as the basis for his analysis of sports and leisure. This theory, which he presents in “The Court Society” uses the nobility’s behavior at the Palace of Versailles to document the creation of the self. According to Elias, the nobles’s shift from rural to court life involved “...more complex relationships that demanded far greater self-control” (Elias 231). Indeed, when the nobility resided on individual estates, they did not have to police their own actions. After assimilating into a highly controlled environment, however, relying on the “old warrior custom” to settle disputes hindered social advancement and in some cases proved life-threatening (Elias 257). As a result of this civilizing process, nobles learned to practice self-awareness and restraint. Elias reasserts this point in “The Quest for Excitement”. When discussing daily routines, Elias argues that these routines “...demand that people keep a fairly firm hold on their moods and drives, their affects and emotions…” (Elias and Dunning 42). By using rigid language like ‘routine’ and ‘firm’, Elias illustrates the extent to which society curbs people’s natural drives. Furthermore, the level of self-policing Elias describes alludes to another consequence of interacting with people and institutions: the development of a consciousness that dictates individual behaviors. Essentially, when people learned to police themselves around others, they started to believe they had a public mask that they presented to others, and a private self existing outside these interactions. In reality, Elias argues, that very sense of self is merely the result of interacting with other people and inhibiting emotions.

    The conscious restriction of emotions leads to the development of an unconscious desire for freedom. According to Elias, “excitement is severely curbed in the pursuit of what one usually regards as the serious business of life” (Elias and Dunning 42). In this quote, ‘excitement’ can refer not only to a state of enthusiasm, but also the expression of spontaneous emotion made impossible by the ‘serious business of life’. ‘Curbing’ these drives, however, does not make them disappear entirely; rather, they get amplified, creating feelings of passion and nostalgia that demand release. This is where sports come into play; when Elias introduces the world of leisure, he explains that these activities allow people “...to flow more freely in an imaginary setting specially created by these activities…” (Elias and Dunning 42). The use of the words ‘flow’ and ‘free’ contrast Elias’ rigid diction in the previous sentence, clearly distinguishing the world of leisure from everyday life and society. Indeed, leisure provides a temporary escape from consciousness and restrictive social norms. For example, when people watch football games, they can openly express anger, elation, and other emotions that people regularly suppress.

    But is leisure actually free? Elias’ argument exposes tensions concerning this point. For example, even if sports allow people to express hidden desires, the 'imaginary setting' created by these activities implies that they are still distancing themselves from emotions by expressing them in a simulated environment. Likewise, when Elias explains that “...the feelings aroused in the imaginary situation of a human leisure activity are the siblings of those aroused in real-life situations…” , he implies that the emotions produced by participating in leisure occupations are related rather than identical (Elias and Dunning 42). This distinction means that leisure activities can “...momentarily lift the burden of risks and threats…surrounding human existence” without any negative consequences, making them extremely appealing (Elias and Dunning 42). People would not yell at their co-workers over a minor dispute if it meant putting their job at risk, but they are completely fine shouting at a television screen when watching a football game. In other words, while the emotions elicited by leisure feel ‘real’, people subconsciously recognize that these scenarios are fake yet nonetheless indulge in them.

    In everyday life, consciousness assumes the role of a supervisor, making sure that a person’s private identity remains hidden under their carefully curated mask. When people engage in leisure activities, that supervisor does not disappear, but instead it becomes a spectator. Ultimately, leisure allows consciousness to take a break and enables people to push the envelope of social norms and express real emotion in an acceptable and inconsequential fashion. While it still constitutes a kind of distancing, recognizing that the world of leisure isn’t real allows us to experience genuine sensations.

    Build 3

    Read Build 3

    In “The Quest for Excitement”, Norbert Elias examines the role of leisure occupations in everyday life. My initial interpretation of this piece discussed how partaking in hobbies like playing sports, reading, and watching television provides people with an escape from strict social conventions and allows them to express emotions they otherwise would suppress. After re-reading this text, as well as “The Court Society”, I realized the flaws in my previous thinking. The purpose of leisure activities, as described by Elias, is not to escape from societal norms, but rather to lessen self-restraint in a controlled manner. This connects to deliberation, the process that occurs when people consider the various outcomes one action could have before making a decision. Deliberation, which is necessary in everyday life, doesn’t disappear in leisure activities, suggesting that the world of leisure is less spontaneous than it appears.

    Elias uses the court society at Versailles to provide a social account of how consciousness develops. He explains that prior to court life, many of the nobles had grown up on large rural estates and came to associate these estates with “...spontaneous simplicity and naturalness” (Elias 231). This association suggests that people had a greater capacity for spontaneity when their interactions were limited to a smaller circle of people, such as their family members. Court life, by contrast, effectively made this kind of spontaneity impossible with its “heavier demands on individual self control” (Elias 231). Elias exemplifies this by talking about Cardinal Richelieu’s approach to duelists. Duelling, previously a hallmark of the “old warrior custom” at rural estates “...was brought to an end when Richelieu had one of the chief duellists of high family publicly executed” (Elias 257). This visible punishment signifies the importance of deliberation in a civilized society. Nobles faced threats to their status and even their lives if they acted in the wrong way, causing them to second-guess themselves and think before acting. As a result of this constant restraint, the nobles envisioned a version of themselves that existed separate from external influences—this vision became “the self”.

    When deliberation becomes a necessity, raw emotion gets suppressed. This is made evident by Elias’s discussion of romantic love. In traditional societies, people were quickly and conveniently arranged into marriages by their family members, with little emphasis placed on emotional connections (Elias 276). In the court, romantic relationships assumed greater significance. Instead of obeying familial mandates, suitors had to follow an “arduous” and “protracted” series of rituals and behaviors to win over the object of their desire (Elias 275). People tend to associate romantic relationships with passion and intimacy, but this account treats love as a form of distancing. Deliberation creates this distance, as people who mull over their actions and determine the best way to win their partner’s affection consequently suppress their immediate drives.

    In a society dictated by self-control, how can people truly express themselves? This is where Elias’s explanation of leisure comes in. According to him, the constant suppression of emotions draws people to sports and leisure, as they provide an outlet for those feelings. This is supported when he writes about how “manifestations of feeling”, or acting upon emotions, is limited in everyday society (Elias 42). Indeed, people stunted by deliberation must confine their true feelings to “special compartments”, whereas leisure activities “appeal to people’s feelings directly” and arouse them (Elias 42). The use of the word ‘arouse’ implies that these activities provide for increased spontaneity. Furthermore, the lack of consequences suggests that people are less likely to think about the effect one action could have. When I first read “The Quest for Entertainment”, I understood this to mean that leisure activities did not require any deliberation–that by partaking in activities like sports and reading, people could finally stop policing themselves for once. What I missed my first time reading was Elias’s use of the term ‘sportization’, a term that argues that there was a “civilizing spurt” in the world of leisure that resembled the ‘courtization’ of the nobles (Elias 151). The development of rules and guidelines shows that leisure cannot be entirely spontaneous, as there are still guidelines for what people can and cannot do. This suggests that deliberation is not absent from leisure activities, but hidden in the background, dictating people’s actions in regards to imaginary consequences. Further support for the claim that leisure isn’t free is Elias’s discussion of mimetic emotions. He describes these emotions as “the siblings of those aroused in real-life situations”, implying that there is a relation between aroused emotions and real ones, but that they are not the same (Elias 42). As a result, leisure acts as a mirror to the real world; it can never be entirely free from the societal structures that shape it. Instead, it is a controlled environment designed to simulate a true alternative.

    When people partake in leisure activities, there is still a level of deliberation that occurs. This deliberation is clearly seen in activities that have rules, like sports or board games, because people still have to operate within that framework and scheme around it. But even activities like reading and drawing, which seem like more passive forms of leisure, require problem solving skills and contemplation. Ultimately, leisure is not escape—much like everything else Elias describes, it is just another type of distancing.

    Build 4

    Objection and Modification

    Read Build 4

    In build three, I claimed that deliberation becomes a necessity as a result of civilizing processes and that this quality does not disappear when people partake in leisure activities. Still, one might argue that the absence of real consequences in the world of leisure makes these activities more spontaneous and therefore less contemplative. While Elias admits that leisure activities give people the feeling of having free rein, these activities do not provide an escape from the constraints of the real world. Instead, sports and leisure activities draw upon that constraint to create an environment where deliberation occurs in regard to imaginary, contained consequences, producing the illusion of freedom.

    In this context, freedom is best understood as an individual’s ability to make their own decisions, irrespective of external influences. This definition of freedom clashes with the reality of everyday life described in “The Court Society”; there, Norbert Elias discusses how the inescapable constraints of court life induce feelings of powerlessness among the nobility, since they must act in accordance with society’s rules (Elias 239). Indeed, Elias contrasts the restrained court society with previous rural lifestyles to show how deliberation replaced spontaneity as people grew increasingly interconnected: “The deliberate sizing up of a situation, the taking of bearings–in short, reflections–intervene more or less automatically between the affective, spontaneous impulse to act and the actual performance of the action in word or deed” (Elias 260). This quote presents reflection as a barrier to freedom, since it acts as a mediator between someone’s impulses and their actual actions.

    In “An Essay on Sport and Violence” Elias seems to argue that leisure activities let people act on those impulses. He achieves this by creating a contrast between daily restraints and leisure by claiming that these activities, “...as a rule allow them to flow more freely in an imaginary setting specially created by these activities” (Elias 42). In build one, I talked about how the words ‘flow’ and ‘free’ suggest that people are liberating themselves from everyday life and its self-constraints. Upon re-reading, I saw that I had missed the word ‘rule’. Elias’s use of this word contradicts his otherwise unrestrained diction by suggesting that people only experience sensations of freedom and release within the context of another structured system. Similarly, Elias mentions that when people interact with others, “the scope for manifestations of feeling is narrowed or confined to special compartments” (Elias 42). This means that society limits one’s ability to act on their emotions, causing them to deliberate and store away those emotions. Again, this points to leisure being ‘freer’, but a closer look begs the question; are leisure activities just another ‘special compartment’ wherein people can release their emotions?

    Elias reveals this is the case by showing how leisure is appealing because its rules make intense emotions safer. In fact, he attributes the emergence of sports in civilized societies to a “...civilizing spurt comparable in its overall direction to the ‘courtization’ of the warriors” (Elias 151). This means that sports are a product of, rather than an alternative to, the everyday rituals of etiquette and self-control. Self constraint and deliberation are still required, albeit to a lesser degree. Take a sport like football, for example. In theory, engaging in this form of leisure allows people to dip into forbidden emotions like violence and anger. But these emotions are produced within the confines of the game—they are ‘mimetic’, and similar in nature, but not real. Additionally, the rules and guidelines associated with a sport like football further hinder authentic self-expression. Players and coaches must plan routes beforehand in order to succeed, and penalties make spontaneous action risky. The risk, however, lies in how those actions affect the scope of the game–the consequences rarely bleed into real life. This controlled setting, with its controlled consequences, is ultimately the very thing that lifts “...the burden of risks and threats, great or small, surrounding human existence” (Elias 42).

    The activities we do in our ‘free time’ are hardly free. While sports and leisure are designed to arouse emotions, these emotions take place in a vacuum that prevents them from being truly authentic. Indeed, deliberation remains a necessity in this setting, but people understand that the consequences they are contemplating are less severe. This has interesting implications for the Westworld theme park, as the feelings and actions portrayed on screen seem real, but nonetheless are stirred by an imaginary setting.

    Build 5

    Compare and Contrast

    Read Build 5

    Elias and Scarry both discuss how people manage intense emotions and experiences by projecting those feelings onto constructed objects and activities. In particular, Elias focuses on the mimetic appeal of leisure, while Scarry explores the extension of sentience onto inanimate objects. Deliberation–the ability to contemplate the various consequences stemming from one action–is implicit in both accounts but manifests itself in different ways. As a sociologist, Elias argues that deliberation exists as a product of long-term social processes. By contrast, Scarry’s object-centered approach focuses on how deliberation precedes projection in the mind of an individual and influences their relationship to objects. Despite these differing interpretations, both authors acknowledge that all forms of expression are bound by notions of pain and restraint.

    According to Elias, deliberation is shaped by environmental pressures. He demonstrates this point using the Seventeenth-Century court at Versailles as a historical case study. His justification for focusing on the nobility is that “negative feelings towards the existing social order…are emotionally localised in certain higher-ranking persons or groups” (Elias 239). This quotation clarifies that the social hierarchy itself doesn’t necessarily create consciousness, but rather the constraints associated with upper-class living make this a particularly self-aware cohort. Indeed, court life under Cardinal Richelieu and Louis XIV was harsh. Dueling, once understood as “...a symbol of individual freedom…that is, the freedom to wound or kill each other if they were so inclined” became taboo under these leaders, as elites faced lethal consequences for engaging in the old warrior custom (Elias 257). The use of the word ‘freedom’ in regards to lost combat traditions emphasizes how absolutist rule hindered the spontaneous expression of intense emotions. Under this system, elites instead had to learn to “duel with words rather than weapons” which “demanded an especially complex self control” (Elias 257). By highlighting the transition from ‘individual freedom’ to ‘self control’, Elias shows how deliberation became necessary as people came into contact with other individuals in a high pressure environment. For the first time, they had to consider what potential consequences there would be if they let their emotions get out of control.

    As a sociologist, Elias’s argument focuses on studying groups of people. To that end, he presents consciousness and deliberation as phenomena formed by, and inseparable from, external factors. Elaine Scarry takes a different approach by focusing on an individual’s internal capacity for empathy and imagination. Her analysis of deliberation is less explicit, but it nonetheless contributes to how people interact with the world, specifically objects, around them. According to her, deliberation is catalyzed by the feeling of pain. This relates to Elias’s nobles, whose fear of punishment under Richelieu and Louis XIV dictated their social behavior. Except instead of focusing on restraint, Scarry suggests that humans respond to pain by reaffirming their existence through projection. Projection, which is the act of extending human sentience onto the external world, causes everyday objects to display “...forms of materialized awareness that go far beyond their most immediate use” (Scarry 305). An aspirin bottle, for instance, has been immediately created to relieve suffering. But in designing that bottle, people have considered whether or not a person can read, what language they are reading in, and how they may screw off the cap. This implies that deliberation precedes projection, as people weigh the different outcomes and the different ways the aspirin bottle can relieve them of their pain.

    Elias presents leisure as a form of projection that allows people to release pent up emotions. The source of those emotions is self-restraint rather than pain, but his discussion of what people do in their free time nonetheless relates to Scarry’s explanation of projection. For example, Elias explains that “...many leisure pursuits provide an imaginary setting which is meant to elicit excitement of some kind imitating that produced by real life situations, yet without its dangers and risks” (Elias 42). In this quote, the lack of urgency allows people to explore different scenarios and dabble in ‘excitement’ mirroring that of real life without endangering themselves. Scarry echoes this point by writing that novels, “...provide this same inanimate fiction of speaking, feeling, thinking, and are perhaps less startling and suspect because we have lived with them a much longer time” (Scarry 305). By using novels as an example of an object that people project themselves onto, Scarry links leisure with the offloading of pain. Both of them discuss the role of mimetic emotions, arguing that the sensations aroused by projection imitate our real experiences. Yet they also understand that projection doesn’t provide people with complete freedom to do what they want. Elias, for example, says that “...game-contests came to embody a rule-set that ensures a balance between the possible attainment of high-combat tension and a reasonable protection against physical injury” (Elias 151), which shows that supposedly spontaneous activities are nonetheless limited by notions of what is and isn’t socially acceptable. Likewise, Scarry cedes that the imagination is mixed up with the phenomenon of compassion” (Scarry 306), showing that when projecting emotions, people are bound by the experience of pain.

    This raises the question: what are the limits of deliberation? How far can a person’s imagination, or a particular leisure activity go? If Elias is correct, and our entire notion of deliberation and the self is shaped by social processes, can we ever think for ourselves without first thinking about other people? Reading Elias and Scarry together shows the limitations of consciousness, even in supposedly free pursuits.
