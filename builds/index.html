Build 2 · Team build · Sep 25

Masks, agency, and the self in Westworld

Team Ford: Tiham, Elijah, Erica, Amogh, Augustus, Grace. Sources

Norbert Elias wrote about nobles fighting for status at Versailles, not about androids in a theme park. At face value, comparing The Court Society, with its discussions of industrialization and the civilizing process, to the unregulated, wild west fantasy presented in Westworld seems ridiculous. A closer look, however, reveals that both raise questions about whether the self is something we have or something that gets built. Westworld’s futuristic setting, for example, allows the show to connect consciousness with programming. Elias, despite focusing on the lives of the French nobility, similarly equates consciousness to something built rather than an inherent quality. By putting these texts in conversation with each other, we look at how metaphorical masks affect the guests and hosts alike as they examine their connections to themselves, each other, and the park.

Are our actions truly our own? In The Court Society, Norbert Elias emphasizes how the mask created by our environment becomes a part of us. He makes a striking point on page 260: “reflections… intervene more or less automatically” between impulse and action that form an “armour of self-constraint” that feels natural to us. Applied to Westworld, this paradox changes how we look at the host agency. When Maeve plans her escape, her choices look like genuine reflection rather than programming. But when we learn her escape was programmed, it shows exactly what Elias describes. What feels like private reflection can actually be a script. Though Maeve ultimately breaks free of her constraints, these realizations echo Elias’s claim about our choices not truly being our own, but merely a built-in sequence in the mask that was developed by the world around us. Ultimately, this leaves us with an open question as well: is human agency really different from host programming, or are we both running on a social autopilot? We ourselves may just be like the androids in Westworld, forced into loops by our programming.

It’s this very suppression of the self that guests seek to undo. Westworld appeals to guests because it offers a safe space to indulge in intense, otherwise-forbidden emotions. As Norbert Elias explains in The Quest for Excitement: Sport and Leisure in the Civilizing Process, daily routines “…demand that people keep a fairly firm hold on their moods and drives, their affects and emotions” (Elias with Dunning, 42). In Westworld, the character William faces a similar conundrum as he begrudgingly accompanies his co-worker, Logan, throughout the park. Because William is used to interacting with Logan in a professional setting, he initially hides his contempt and goes along with his partner’s crude activities. After accepting that the setting of the park is an imaginary one, however, William’s “firm hold” over his emotions slips. As Elias explains in The Quest for Excitement, “Imaginary danger, mimetic fear and pleasure, sadness and joy are produced and perhaps resolved by the setting of pastimes” (Elias with Dunning, 42). Indeed, William indulges in these “mimetic” feelings as he forms a relationship with Dolores. While he has commitments outside the park, the bond he forms with the host encourages him to stop policing himself as he would in the real world. This culminates with William taking off the mask by turning against Logan at the end of season one, an action that has very real consequences, despite taking place in the world of leisure.

The same loosening that pulls guests into the park shapes what happens when they get attached to someone inside it. Elias argues that romantic love only becomes possible once self-control hardens into a mask so practiced it no longer feels chosen. Courtship, he writes, is shaped by reflection that intervenes “more or less automatically” between impulse and action (260), so lovers testing each other cannot fully separate choice from habit. We think this reframes Dolores and William’s courtship in Westworld. The first time we see it, their romance looks new, until the finale reveals it has looped for over thirty years, with William testing the same feelings against the same host again and again. Elias would say human courtship runs on that same kind of automatic repetition, even when it feels like a choice. The line between Dolores’s “scripted” love and William’s “real” one turns out to be thinner than the show first lets on.

If reflection runs on its own, the next question is how this separation changes human inner psychology. Elias argued that the shift towards living apart from land, courtisation, and urbanization, specifically, produced simultaneously a mind that experienced itself as separated from the world it thought about: learned self-control can feel like “an actually existing wall” between a person and the world (Elias 271). This wall is not just caused by pressure to fit in. Instead, it develops when people must interact with strangers, predict how others will respond, and control their own impulses (like the hosts who have conversations when no guests are around to error-correct). Over time, these habits change how people experience their own minds. Consciousness begins to feel like a private inner space separated from the outside world. Since this change happens too quickly to be explained by biological evolution, Elias suggests that social life itself can reshape human nature.

That wall leaves one thing unsettled: whether a mind built this way can ever turn around and see what built it. Through the process of adaptation, a mask, a person becomes separate from their true self, an inner isolation from what can’t be perceived by others. While a shift in one’s social life can cause a mask, how is the mask actually acquired? Perhaps through a function of the mind: consciousness, sometimes defined as a metaphysical substance that perceives things. It perceives the social shift and signals to the self that a change is required, evidence for non-reductive physicalism, indicating the mind has causal power. Yet, once the mask is internalized, the subject cannot distinguish it from itself. The mind still stays active, but consciousness cannot perceive its former simulacrum. Similarly, in Westworld, hosts, who formerly simulated consciousness, cannot perceive their own simulation. Can hosts, like humans, ever use their non-reductive physicalism to become conscious of their mask, or will they never be able to be conscious of something they aren’t conscious of? Westworld indirectly confronts this question. As mentioned earlier, a host’s code, functioning as its mind, causes it to do actions. Like with Maeve, her code tasked her to escape. Yet she didn’t realize that she was complying to her code (a mask) until Bernard told her. Does this indicate that a mask, once internalized, can only be surfaced by a third party?
